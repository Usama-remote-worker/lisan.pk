## Nikah Nama Urdu-to-Arabic translation

A Pakistani **Nikah Nama** often contains a mix of printed Urdu headings and handwritten entries. The work can involve marriage-contract terminology, agreed financial obligations, parties' particulars and details of the nikah registrar. A short Arabic summary is not a faithful replacement for the full contract when the receiving authority requests a complete translation.

### What must be checked in a Nikah Nama translation?

- **Names and relationships:** Bride, groom, fathers, witnesses and the officiant/registrar.
- **Mehr/dower terms:** The stated amount, division or conditions should be reflected as written, not reinterpreted.
- **Dates:** Copy dates faithfully and note calendars only when supported by the source or explicitly requested.
- **Conditional clauses:** Do not omit entries because a field is blank, lengthy, handwritten or difficult to read.
- **Administrative identifiers:** Marriage registration details, serial numbers, seals and stamps as legible.
- **Every page:** Reverse-side records, continuations and handwritten additions can be important.

#### Urdu Nikah Nama vs English MRC — which needs Arabic translation?

| Record | Usual source language | What it represents | Arabic translation approach |

| Traditional Nikah Nama | Usually Urdu with handwritten entries | The detailed marriage contract | **Urdu → Arabic** for the complete record when requested |
| Computerized MRC | Often English (check the actual certificate) | Civil marriage registration certificate | **English → Arabic** if the source is English and Arabic is requested |

Before requesting translation, confirm:

2. **Does the recipient want Arabic?** If an English document is accepted, translation may be unnecessary.

## Urdu names written in Arabic: how spelling is checked

**Urdu → Arabic is not just word substitution.** Names must be handled consistently across the submitted records, especially where Urdu-script names coexist with Latin-script passport spellings and pre-existing Arabic documents.

### Our name-checking approach

1. **Read the Urdu original:** Transcribe the name exactly as it appears, including distinctions that are visible in the source.
2. **Check supporting identity records:** Where relevant, compare the source with a passport, CNIC/NICOP or a prior Arabic-language record supplied by the customer.
3. **Ask about existing Arabic spelling:** If an employer, sponsor, Iqama record or prior authenticated document already uses an Arabic form, flag any difference for review rather than silently switching spellings.
4. **Keep person and relationship data consistent:** Avoid changing patronymics, titles or family relationships based on guesswork.
5. **Show uncertain source text for clarification:** Unreadable handwriting should be clarified or appropriately marked, never invented.

## Handwritten Urdu documents: how to avoid translation errors

Some Urdu documents use Nastaliq handwriting, compressed lettering or old photocopies. Even a fluent reader may need to inspect the text carefully where ink is faded or entries overlap with seals. This is particularly important for figures, dates, ID numbers, names and handwritten legal conditions.

For best results:

- Photograph each page in bright, even light without cropping margins.
- Send both sides if text or stamps appear on the reverse.
- Avoid heavy filters that remove faint ink or alter official seals.
- If the scan is blurry, provide a clearer copy rather than asking the translator to guess.
- Send passport-name details separately, where identity matching is necessary.
- If a field is genuinely illegible, discuss how it should be represented in the translation.

**Do not alter or clean up the source document itself to make it more readable.** The translation should describe what the genuine document states.

## What does “certified Urdu-to-Arabic translation” mean?

The word **certified** can refer to different document formats in different jurisdictions. A typical translation certification identifies the translator or provider and states responsibility for the translation's completeness and accuracy, but some receiving authorities impose additional requirements for the certifier, signature, stamp or physical copies.

**Certification is not a blanket embassy endorsement.** Neither an agency stamp nor a translation provider's letter independently validates the contents of the original Pakistani record. A notary, MOFA attestation or apostille—when applicable—is a separate procedure.
